Ecosystem

Five roles,one contract.

PRE-GEN works because each participant does one thing well. The protocol defines the rules. A registry operator runs the infrastructure. Copyright owners set the terms. AI providers ask before they generate. Creative studios get content with its rights attached. Here is exactly what each role does — and what it gets back.

The roles.

THE PROTOCOL

PRE-GEN

Role: the rulebook. Defines PG identifiers, the five signed objects (license, decision, receipt, evidence bundle, assertion), and byte-level test vectors every implementation must pass.

What it gives everyone: one contract. Any registry, any provider, any studio — same rules, same verification, no bespoke integrations. Open specification under Apache-2.0, owned by no one.

REGISTRY OPERATORS

PRAMPTA & other registries

Role: run the infrastructure. Verify rights-holder identity, assign PG codes, sign every allow/deny decision, keep the hash-chained audit log, resolve disputes, issue provider credentials.

What they give: nobody has to run a server to use the protocol — rights holders register, providers get keys, studios get evidence. PRAMPTA is the first commercial operator; the specification is operator-agnostic, and any company may run one.

COPYRIGHT OWNERS

Actors, musicians, brands, estates

Role: the source of every permission. Register a subject once, set the terms — tiers, scopes, prices — or file a free unconditional opt-out.

What they get: every compliant AI asks before it generates. Authorized uses pay on their terms; unauthorized uses are provable. Protection is not a premium feature — saying “never generate me” is free forever.

AI PROVIDERS

Model & platform companies

Role: the ones who must ask. Image, video, and voice generators integrate the three-call flow — detect subjects in prompts, verify, keep the signed decision.

What they get: a legal shield three calls deep — per-generation proof of authorization any court can verify independently, structured PG_* refusals instead of silent risk, and the audit trail the EU-AI-Act era demands. Sandbox-first: Python & TypeScript SDKs.

CREATIVE STUDIOS

Film, TV, advertising, games

Role: the ones who ship AI content into the world. Their pipelines call PRE-GEN before generating, collect the signed objects per scene, and export evidence bundles into production paperwork.

What they get: chain of title on autopilot. E&O insurers and distributors receive documentation, not promises — and content stays shippable as disclosure rules tighten.

ANYONE

Verifiers

Role: check the work. Platforms, insurers, courts, journalists — anyone can resolve a PG code at /lookup, verify signatures against public keys, and walk the audit chain offline.

What they get: trust that doesn’t depend on anyone’s word. The math and the audit chain are the witness.

How it works together.

One scene in a film, generated with a licensed actor’s likeness. Six moves, five signed objects, zero ambiguity afterwards.

01
Register. The actor registers a subject with the registry. PG code PG-000042* assigned — permanent, check-summed, cited forever.
02
License. The production requests a license; the actor approves. Scoped: film title, territory, media, time window. Signed by the owner, countersigned by the registry. pg.license.v2
03
Ask. The studio’s generative pipeline detects the subject in a prompt and calls /v1/verify/. A signed decision comes back: allowed. pg.decision.v1
04
Generate & prove. The scene is produced; the provider files a receipt tying the output to the decision. pg.receipt.v2
05
Attach. A PRE-GEN assertion is embedded into the scene’s C2PA manifest — the permission travels inside the file. pg.assertion.v1
06
Clear. The studio exports the evidence bundle — decision, license, audit chain — into the chain-of-title file for insurers and distributors. pg.evidence.v1

Why PRE-GEN exists.

Generation got cheap. Permission didn’t. An AI-generated face, voice, or brand in a film is a legal event: someone owns that likeness, and “the model made it” has never been a defense.

Until now there was no standard way to ask, grant, and prove permission for one specific generation. Contracts live in inboxes. Consent disputes live in court. The burden of proof lands on whoever generated — months later, with nothing but a vendor’s word.

PRE-GEN makes permission a protocol: asked by machines, granted on the owner’s terms, verifiable by anyone — before the pixel exists, and for decades after.

Why the split between spec and operator matters.

PRE-GEN is an open specification; PRAMPTA is its first commercial registry. That separation is deliberate. If a studio’s legal department distrusts one operator, the protocol outlives that distrust — anyone can run a competing registry, and the signed objects verify the same way. Trust attaches to signatures and audit chains, not to a company’s word.
